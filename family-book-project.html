Purpose and choice

Analyze one family through a sociological lens

Choose one family to use throughout the project. You may examine your own family or a family you know well enough to discuss accurately. Your slides should connect that family’s experiences to culture, social structure, institutions, inequality, policy, technology, historical change, and the life course.

Your own family
You may use your family of origin, current household, chosen family, or another family network to which you belong.
A family you know
You may use relatives, close friends, neighbors, or another family whose experiences you understand. Protect their privacy and avoid assumptions.
Privacy and safety: You never have to reveal trauma, abuse, diagnoses, illegal behavior, financial details, or other information that could harm or identify someone. Change names, omit identifying details, and choose a different family experience when necessary. Do not diagnose people or make unsupported allegations.
The slide recipe

Use this structure for every section slide

1. Focused title
Name the family pattern or question you are examining.
2. Family example
Choose your own family or a family you know. Change names and identifying details when privacy requires it.
3. Sociological analysis
Write approximately 100–150 words in your own words and accurately apply at least two bolded course concepts.
4. Institutional connection
Explain how law, work, school, media, healthcare, policy, religion, technology, or another institution shapes the example.
5. Required image
Every section slide must include an image that represents its main idea. You may use a personal photo, properly sourced image, chart, diagram, or AI-generated image. Add alternative text and cite the source or identify it as AI-generated.
6. Citation
Cite the coursebook and any outside facts, images, quotations, statistics, or public cases used on the slide.
Eight coursebook connections

Choose one prompt for each section

1. Family as a Social Institution

Choose one

  • Create a family snapshot showing structure, roles, residence, authority, kinship, or chosen family.
  • Compare an earlier family generation with family life today and connect the change to policy, technology, work, education, or a major event.
  • Explain how culture and racial or ethnic diversity shape family practices without treating difference as deficiency.

Possible concepts: social institution, socialization, fictive kin, authority, residence, ideal culture, real culture, intersectionality.

Review Section One

2. Studying Families

Choose one

  • Turn a family topic into a research question and select the best method, sample, and evidence.
  • Improve a biased, unreliable, or invalid survey question about families.
  • Apply one micro-level and one macro-level theory to the same family pattern, showing what each reveals.

Possible concepts: reliability, validity, sample, correlation, causation, ethics, symbolic interactionism, conflict theory, functionalism, feminist theory.

Review Section Two

3. Socialization and Gender

Choose one

  • Trace one gender message through family, school, and media and explain whether the sources reinforce or challenge one another.
  • Analyze how gender roles influence household labor, caregiving, identity, opportunity, or power.
  • Use a historical or public case to distinguish biological variation, gender identity, gender expression, and society’s classifications.

Possible concepts: sex traits, gender, gender socialization, stereotype, ideology, gender spectrum, patriarchy, stratification.

Review Section Three

4. The Economy and Family Life

Choose one

  • Show how inflation, housing, childcare, healthcare, education, or technology costs affect a household budget and family choices.
  • Analyze how social class and wealth—not income alone—shape family opportunity and security.
  • Compare U.S. family supports with another industrialized country and explain what taxes provide in return.

Possible concepts: social class, wealth, poverty, unpaid labor, work–family conflict, social safety net, inflation, OECD.

Review Section Four

5. Relationships and Communication

Choose one

  • Use filter theory to explain how social location and opportunity shape who is likely to meet and form a relationship.
  • Apply love styles or communication patterns to a fictional, public, or personal relationship.
  • Explain why relationship satisfaction may change across the life course and identify realistic strategies for a demanding stage.

Possible concepts: singlehood, cohabitation, homogamy, propinquity, filter theory, love styles, conflict pattern, marital satisfaction.

Review Section Five

6. Family Formation, Parenting, and Development

Choose one

  • Compare two legitimate family-formation pathways, including resources, opportunities, policies, and ethical questions.
  • Apply one child-development theory and one parenting or attachment concept to a realistic example.
  • Explain how biology and social experience interact in development without treating genes or environment as destiny.

Possible concepts: reproductive justice, kinship care, attachment, parenting styles, Mead, Erikson, Piaget, Bronfenbrenner, Bandura, epigenetics.

Review Section Six

7. Family Challenges, Stress, Abuse, and Violence

Choose one

  • Use the Double ABC-X or family-resilience framework to show how demands, meanings, and supports shape a family crisis.
  • Explain how social institutions can reduce stress for a family managing disability, mental-health needs, caregiving, or postpartum illness.
  • Analyze where a harmful cycle can be interrupted through safety, accountability, treatment, skills, policy, and community support.

Possible concepts: stressor pile-up, appraisal, resilience, role overload, coercive control, trauma-informed support, social learning, cycle breaking.

Review Section Seven

8. Family Change Across the Life Course

Choose one

  • Map how divorce, remarriage, or stepfamily formation changes roles, boundaries, residence, and kinship.
  • Apply a later-life theory to retirement, grandparenting, caregiving, social activity, or continuity of identity.
  • Identify an ageist assumption and explain how attitudes can become institutional discrimination.

Possible concepts: life-course theory, linked lives, stepfamily, kin keeper, activity theory, continuity theory, disengagement theory, social exchange, ageism.

Review Section Eight
Artificial intelligence policy

AI may support your learning, but it may not write the project

You remain the author of every sentence submitted for the Family Book Project. The analysis must reflect your own understanding of the course and the family you selected.

Permitted AI use
You may use AI to check grammar after you write, create or edit a representative image, explain a difficult course concept, or help you study your instructor’s feedback.
Prohibited AI use
Do not ask AI to write, rewrite, paraphrase, expand, summarize, or generate any slide content. Do not submit AI-created analysis, examples, reflections, takeaways, citations, or speaker notes as your work.
Your responsibility
Verify every concept, fact, and citation. Protect private family information. Follow your instructor’s directions for identifying AI-generated images or other permitted assistance.
AI image note: If AI creates an image, label it in the caption as an AI-generated illustration and briefly describe the prompt or tool according to your instructor’s citation directions. Never use AI to create a deceptive image of a real family member or event.
Final assembly

Your completed Family Book

  1. Cover slide: project title, your name, course, and a meaningful visual.
  2. Eight section slides: one completed slide for each coursebook section.
  3. Final synthesis slide: state one key takeaway from each section, then identify two ideas or pieces of advice you want to carry forward.
  4. References slide or slides: list the coursebook and every outside source in APA style.

Your final submission contains at least 11 slides. Additional slides are welcome when needed for readability, visuals, or references.

APA and source use

Cite ideas as well as quotations

Paraphrase most information in your own words and provide a brief citation on the slide. Include the complete entry on the final References slide.

3.1 Sex, gender, and identity

What is determined at conception—and what is not?

In the typical XX/XY pathway, the egg contributes an X chromosome and the fertilizing sperm contributes either X or Y. The sperm’s chromosome therefore establishes the embryo’s usual XX or XY chromosomal pattern. This concerns chromosomal sex, not a person’s future gender identity, personality, social role, or worth.

Each conception is a new probability; previous children do not control the next outcome.
Important variation: Not everyone has a typical XX or XY pattern, and chromosomes do not always predict anatomy in a simple way. Variations can include XXY, XO, mosaic patterns, androgen insensitivity, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, and other differences of sex development. “Typically” is therefore more accurate than “always.”

Sex and gender are related, but they are not synonyms

Sex characteristics
Chromosomes, genes, hormones, gonads, reproductive anatomy, and secondary physical traits.
Gender identity
A person’s internal understanding of their gender. It cannot be read directly from a chromosome image.
Gender expression
How people communicate gender through appearance, language, behavior, and other social signs.
Gender roles and ideology
Social expectations about how people categorized as women, men, girls, boys, or other genders should behave and what power they should hold.
Chromosome chart

Beyond XX and XY: variation in the 23rd pair

Most human body cells contain 46 chromosomes arranged in 23 pairs—not 23 chromosomes total. A trisomy means three copies of a chromosome; a monosomy means one copy instead of the usual pair.

47,XXY — Klinefelter syndrome
An extra X chromosome affects male development. Features vary and can be mild. XXY alone does not mean a person has “both male and female parts.”
47,XXX — trisomy X
Girls and women have an additional X chromosome. Some have no apparent features; others experience developmental, learning, or health differences.
47,XYY — XYY syndrome
An additional Y chromosome affects males. Many have few unusual physical features, while development and learning can vary.
45,X — Turner syndrome
One X chromosome is missing or altered in cells. Turner syndrome affects female development and is a monosomy, not a trisomy.
Chromosomes are not destiny. Sex development also involves genes, hormones, receptors, gonads, anatomy, and development over time. “Intersex” or “differences of sex development” includes many distinct variations; it is not another name for XXY alone.

Anne Fausto-Sterling and the “five sexes” argument

Biologist and gender-studies scholar Anne Fausto-Sterling’s 1993 essay used a deliberately provocative five-category model to challenge the idea that every body fits neatly into two biological boxes. Its sociological value lies in asking who creates categories, whose bodies become the standard, and what institutions do when bodies do not fit.

The model is not a current clinical classification. Some historical labels in the essay are now dated and stigmatizing, so this coursebook does not use them to name people. Current teaching should identify a specific variation when relevant, use person-centered language, and distinguish biological diversity from gender identity and society’s rules for classifying bodies.

Case study • socialization, identity, and ethics

David Reimer and the “John/Joan” case

Content note: This case involves childhood medical injury, nonconsensual treatment, coercive clinical practices, psychological trauma, and suicide. The account avoids graphic detail and centers David Reimer’s humanity rather than treating him as an experiment.

The case is historically important because it challenged a strong version of the claim that gender identity could be produced almost entirely through socialization. It is equally important as a warning about medical authority, secrecy, irreversible interventions, and research conducted without meaningful consent or respect for the child.

What happened?

1965–1966David Reimer was born in Winnipeg as one of identical twin boys. At approximately eight months, his penis was severely injured during a circumcision procedure.
Medical recommendationPsychologist John Money advised the family to reassign and raise David as a girl. His parents followed expert advice during a period when clinicians often believed early rearing could determine gender identity.
ChildhoodDavid was given a female name and social role and received medical treatment intended to support the reassignment. He resisted the assigned identity and experienced significant distress. Clinical sessions later described by David and his twin also raise serious concerns about coercion and abuse.
AdolescenceAt about age 14, his parents told him the history of the injury and reassignment. He chose the name David, lived as male, and later received medical care aligned with that identity.
Public record and legacyMilton Diamond and H. Keith Sigmundson published a long-term follow-up in 1997 that contradicted earlier reports of success. David later chose to tell his story publicly so clinicians would reconsider similar treatment.

Why was it called the “John/Joan” case?

Early professional reports protected the subject’s identity by using pseudonyms such as “John/Joan.” Those reports portrayed the reassignment as successful and were widely cited in arguments that gender identity was highly malleable in early childhood. David’s later testimony and the 1997 follow-up showed that this account omitted or misinterpreted crucial evidence of resistance and distress.

What the case can support

  • Gender identity cannot safely be assumed to be infinitely malleable through parenting and social reinforcement.
  • Children are people with developing agency, not blank slates or research materials.
  • Patients and families need truthful information, meaningful consent, age-appropriate assent, independent support, and long-term follow-up.
  • Irreversible interventions demand particular caution when they are not medically urgent and the person cannot participate in the decision.
  • Researchers must report outcomes honestly, including evidence that challenges their theory.

What the case cannot prove by itself

Overstated claim More careful conclusion
“The case proves gender is entirely biological.” No single case can establish how biology, development, relationships, culture, and self-understanding interact for everyone.
“Socialization does not matter.” Socialization strongly shapes gender roles, expression, opportunity, language, and treatment. The case challenges the idea that socialization can simply manufacture any identity professionals choose.
“David’s experience is the same as being transgender or intersex.” No. David was born with a typical XY pattern and was reassigned following an injury. His circumstances should not be used to invalidate transgender identities or to collapse all differences of sex development into one story.
“His suicide had one proven cause.” David died by suicide in 2004 after multiple documented stresses. It is inappropriate to reduce suicide to a single cause or use his death as proof of one theory.

Watch and reflect

Family, medicine, research, education, media, and gender norms all played roles. His parents’ choices also occurred within a major power imbalance: they relied on respected experts who controlled specialized knowledge.
Professional authority influenced the family’s decisions, the information they received, David’s treatment, and the published scientific account. David had the least institutional power even though the decisions affected his body and life.
It would provide honest developmentally appropriate information, listen to persistent distress, seek assent when possible, avoid unnecessary irreversible procedures, offer independent counseling, preserve future options, and revise the plan as the child’s own identity becomes clearer.
Animated evidence check

Cycle tracking and attempted sex selection

Some people track cervical mucus, basal body temperature, or ovulation tests to estimate the fertile window. That can help identify when conception is more likely. Popular timing methods go further and claim that intercourse earlier or later in the window can favor X-bearing or Y-bearing sperm.

X-bearing sperm
Y-bearing sperm
Popular theory versus evidence: both move together because the “slow, durable X versus fast, fragile Y” race is not established science. Egg position does not reliably select one chromosome.
What students should remember: cycle tracking may help estimate ovulation and the chance of pregnancy. It has not been shown to reliably determine whether an X-bearing or Y-bearing sperm fertilizes the egg. These are chromosome-bearing sperm—not “female sperm” and “male sperm”—and chromosomal sex is not a child’s future gender.

From private preference to a population pattern

When a society gives sons greater inheritance, economic, ritual, or old-age-support value, individual preferences can combine with prenatal sex determination, discriminatory practices, or restrictive population policies. The result can be a sex ratio at birth far above the biological norm. The sociological cause is not “too many boys”; it is the unequal valuation and treatment of girls and women.

How imbalance develops
Son preference + access to sex selection + economic or policy pressure can turn family-level decisions into a population-level skew.
Possible social consequences
Large cohorts of men facing a shortage of potential female partners can coincide with marriage-market pressure, trafficking, forced marriage, exploitation, and intensified control of women. These are institutional risks—not automatic behavior by individual men.

A “balanced” society does not require governments or families to engineer births. A rights-based response removes discrimination: value children equally, strengthen women’s education and economic security, reform unequal inheritance and care systems, and protect reproductive autonomy.

It can. Tracking can help estimate the fertile window, although bodies and cycles vary.
No reliable evidence supports that explanation. Egg position is not a dependable sex-selection mechanism, and research has not shown intercourse timing to influence a baby’s sex in practice.
Because gender inequality can shape repeated decisions across millions of families. Institutions, technology, law, inheritance, and economic insecurity can transform a private preference into discrimination and demographic imbalance.
Evidence check

The “fast Y sperm and slow X sperm” claim

The terms endosperm and gynosperm are not accepted names for human X- and Y-bearing sperm. Endosperm is plant tissue that nourishes a developing seed. In humans, the relevant terms are X-bearing sperm and Y-bearing sperm.

The popular Shettles method claimed that Y-bearing sperm were faster but more fragile while X-bearing sperm were slower and longer-lived. It therefore proposed timing intercourse near ovulation for a boy and earlier for a girl. Well-designed research has not shown that intercourse timing relative to ovulation has a practical influence on a baby’s sex. Timing can affect the probability of conception because pregnancy is more likely during the fertile window; it should not be presented as a reliable way to select XX or XY.

Too absolute. A Y chromosome usually initiates a pathway associated with testes development, but genes, hormones, receptors, anatomy, and developmental variation mean chromosomes alone do not describe every person’s sex characteristics—and they do not determine gender identity.
Accurate for the typical pathway. The egg contributes X; the sperm contributes X or Y. “Contributes” is better than “fault,” because an individual does not consciously select which sperm fertilizes an egg.
Not supported. Timing intercourse can affect whether conception occurs, but research does not support using timing as a dependable method of selecting a child’s chromosomal sex.
False. Gender roles are learned and enforced through socialization, institutions, culture, rewards, sanctions, and power. Biology does not dictate that one gender must rule, inherit, earn, care, or obey.
Historical case study

Henry VIII, his wives, and the politics of producing a son

Henry VIII’s succession crisis demonstrates how limited biological knowledge can combine with patriarchy, monarchy, religion, and inheritance rules to assign blame.

Henry wanted a surviving legitimate son to secure the Tudor succession. Catherine of Aragon and Anne Boleyn experienced pregnancies that did not produce the surviving male heir Henry demanded, and both women were subjected to political and personal blame. Jane Seymour gave birth to Edward VI in 1537 and died shortly afterward. Modern genetics reveals the irony: in the typical XX/XY pathway, the fertilizing sperm—not the egg—contributes the chromosome that establishes XX or XY.

But “the father was at fault” still uses the wrong sociological frame. Neither parent controls which individual sperm fertilizes an egg, and pregnancy loss, infertility, and fetal development can involve many factors. The deeper problem was a social system that treated women’s value as dependent on producing a male heir while concentrating dynastic authority and inheritance in men.

Apply the sociological imagination

Culture
A male heir was associated with political continuity and military stability.
Institution
Monarchy, inheritance law, marriage, and church authority turned reproduction into a matter of state.
Gender ideology
Women were expected to produce heirs yet lacked control over chromosomes, succession rules, or many decisions about their marriages.
Power
Henry could seek annulments, imprison opponents, and punish wives; the women did not possess equivalent power to redefine the situation.

Interactive: move from individual blame to social analysis

Because patriarchal gender ideology treated reproduction as a wife’s duty, medical knowledge was limited, and political institutions made the birth of a son unusually consequential.
In the typical XX/XY pathway, the egg provides X while the sperm provides X or Y. A wife does not biologically determine whether the embryo receives an X or Y sperm.
Sociology asks why producing a male heir mattered so much, why women carried the blame, how law and religion reinforced that blame, and why one person possessed the power to impose severe consequences.
3.2 Learning gender: socialization and development

How do people learn gender?

Gender socialization is the lifelong process through which people learn the meanings, expectations, opportunities, and boundaries attached to gender categories. Children are not empty containers: they observe patterns, test rules, notice inconsistencies, and actively build an understanding of themselves.

Three major institutional sources

1. Family: the first messages

Before children enter school or choose media, families communicate gender through names, clothing, toys, room colors, chores, affection, discipline, language, and the division of paid and unpaid work they observe.

Repeated messages can embed bias—for example, comforting girls while telling boys to suppress fear—or provide guidance that emphasizes shared human abilities, respects differences, and allows variation.

2. School: rules and opportunity

Schools teach through curricula, teacher expectations, dress codes, sports, bathrooms, peer cultures, disciplinary practices, and whose accomplishments appear in lessons. The hidden curriculum includes lessons conveyed by routines even when they are not formally taught.

Schools can reproduce inequality or interrupt it by presenting varied role models, responding consistently to harassment, and giving all students access to leadership, care, arts, technology, and athletics.

3. Media: stories and visibility

Television, games, music, advertising, news, influencers, and algorithms model which bodies, occupations, relationships, and emotions appear normal or valuable. Repetition can make a social arrangement look natural.

Media also expands possibilities. Seeing women as scientists, nurturing men, LGBTQ+ families, and gender-diverse people can challenge narrow scripts and supply language for experiences a child already recognizes.

Peers matter too. Friends, siblings, teammates, and online communities often reward conformity or sanction difference. This chapter features family, school, and media as three central institutions, but peer groups are powerful socializing agents that connect all three.

Gender is not one switch

Societies often package gender as two fixed opposites: masculine or feminine, strong or caring, leader or helper. Human lives do not sort so neatly.

A spectrum is more accurate when it means multidimensional variation. Identity, expression, interests, roles, bodies, and relationships are separate dimensions—not a single scale with “male” at one end and “female” at the other. Some people experience gender as stable; others experience it as fluid, mixed, neutral, or outside binary categories. Respecting variation does not require denying average differences or forcing anyone to change how they understand themselves.

Four ways socialization operates

Modeling
Children observe who cooks, earns, repairs, comforts, leads, interrupts, or receives respect.
Reinforcement
Praise, teasing, attention, punishment, and exclusion make some behaviors easier and others costly.
Channeling
Adults and institutions offer different toys, courses, sports, clothing, responsibilities, and futures.
Language and classification
Labels such as “bossy,” “tomboy,” “ladylike,” or “man up” attach gendered meanings to behavior.

Interactive: identify the socializing process

Modeling and counter-socialization. The child sees care as a human responsibility rather than women’s work.
Channeling and differential expectations. Even without stating a stereotype, attention can shape confidence, practice, and future course choices.
Algorithmic reinforcement. Prior clicks and commercial categories can narrow what users see, making gendered patterns appear to reflect preference alone.
Agency and resistance. Socialization is powerful but never total. People interpret and sometimes challenge the messages they receive.
3.3 Gender roles, stereotypes, and ideology

From repeated expectations to “common sense”

A gender role is a social expectation; a stereotype is an oversimplified belief about a category; and gender ideology is the larger belief system used to explain or justify how responsibilities, authority, and rewards should be divided.

Repeated message
Stereotype appears natural
Institutions organize around it
Unequal outcome seems deserved

Descriptive and prescriptive stereotypes

Descriptive stereotypes claim what women, men, or gender-diverse people supposedly are like. Prescriptive stereotypes state how they should behave. A woman may be stereotyped as emotional; if she acts assertively, she may then be sanctioned for violating the expectation that she should be agreeable. Men can be punished for vulnerability or care work because masculinity is often policed through other men’s approval.

Hegemonic masculinity
A culturally dominant ideal that connects “real manhood” with authority, heterosexuality, toughness, control, and economic success. Most men do not fully embody it, yet it can still organize status among men.
Emphasized femininity
An ideal that rewards accommodation to men’s interests through appearance, nurturance, deference, and heterosexual desirability. Femininities also vary by class, race, age, sexuality, disability, and culture.
Doing gender
People produce gender in everyday interaction—through dress, speech, emotion, work, posture, and accountability to others’ expectations. It is something people accomplish, not merely something they possess.
Intersectionality
Gender never acts alone. Race, class, sexuality, citizenship, age, and disability combine to shape which versions of femininity or masculinity are rewarded, punished, or even available.

Important distinction: Matrilineal means descent, inheritance, or kinship is traced through the mother’s line; matrifocal means family life centers strongly on women or mothers. Historical case: Victorian “separate spheres”

A powerful nineteenth-century middle-class ideal associated men with the public sphere—paid work, politics, rationality, and authority—and women with the private sphere—home, morality, emotion, nurturance, and deference. This was an ideology, not a description of every household: many women worked for wages, and class, race, empire, and marital status shaped who could approximate the ideal. Its importance lies in how it made a particular division of labor appear natural.

Reflect: If leadership, courage, technical skill, caregiving, cooperation, and emotional intelligence are useful human capacities, what is lost when a society assigns each capacity to only one gender? How could families prepare every child to practice all of them?

3.4 Gender inequality, power, and stratification

How small patterns accumulate into unequal lives

Stratification means patterned inequality among social categories. Gender stratification appears when gender affects income, wealth, time, safety, political voice, education, occupational authority, health, or freedom—even when no single person openly intends discrimination.

The gendered division of labor

Paid employment and family labor are interdependent. Meals, cleaning, emotional support, transportation, appointments, child development, and elder care reproduce the labor force and sustain communities, yet much of this work is unpaid or poorly paid. Globally, women and girls perform substantially more unpaid care work than men. The result can be a time penalty: fewer uninterrupted hours for education, sleep, advancement, political participation, or leisure.

Second shift
Household and care work performed after paid employment.
Mental load
Remembering, anticipating, scheduling, noticing, and coordinating what a household needs.
Occupational segregation
Women and men are concentrated in different jobs, industries, and levels of authority.
Glass ceiling and glass escalator
Invisible barriers can slow women’s advancement, while men in some women-dominated occupations may be moved toward authority.

Interactive: make invisible time visible

Person A performs 14 more hours each week—about 728 hours across a year.

The calculator does not decide what is fair. Families may divide labor differently because of employment hours, disability, skill, preference, culture, or life stage. The sociological questions are whether the arrangement is visible, negotiated, revisable, and connected to equal rest, decision-making, and future opportunity.

Power is more than who “wins” an argument

Resource power
Control over income, property, transportation, healthcare, technology, or legal status.
Agenda power
Control over which problems are discussed and which are dismissed as private or unimportant.
Definition power
The ability to name behavior normal, deviant, professional, emotional, safe, or dangerous.
Institutional power
Rules and routines that advantage some groups even without an openly prejudiced decision-maker.

Families can interrupt inequality

Families prepare children for adult society not by teaching one rigid script, but by teaching competence: every child can learn care, communication, boundary-setting, financial literacy, household labor, emotional regulation, technology, leadership, and respect. When children see work shared and people redirected without humiliation, they learn that equality is a daily practice rather than only an abstract belief.

3.5 Global gender issues

Compare systems without ranking cultures

Gender is global, but it is never identical everywhere. Marriage, inheritance, work, care, education, dress, sexuality, and political authority are shaped by local history as well as colonialism, religion, markets, migration, conflict, social movements, law, and technology.

A sociological global lens

Education: Gender gaps differ by level and place. Poverty, conflict, caregiving, safety, school costs, disability access, and local labor markets may disadvantage girls in one context and boys in another. Enrollment alone does not reveal curriculum bias, harassment, learning, completion, or opportunity after graduation.

Four safeguards against ethnocentrism

Look within societies
Urban and rural regions, classes, ethnic groups, generations, and families may differ sharply.
Look historically
Practices change. Today’s arrangement is not an ancient or inevitable cultural essence.
Follow power and resources
Ask who benefits, who pays, what alternatives exist, and how law or markets constrain choice.
Listen to local actors
People inside a society debate, resist, defend, and transform gender norms; they are not passive examples.

Technology can reproduce or challenge inequality

Digital work, online education, telehealth, and social networks can expand opportunity. The same technologies can intensify surveillance, harassment, misinformation, algorithmic bias, and unequal access. Families therefore prepare children for gender equality partly through digital literacy: questioning recommendations, protecting privacy, recognizing harassment, evaluating sources, and refusing to confuse popularity with truth.

Global does not mean “somewhere else.” The United States is part of the comparison. Analyzing other societies without examining one’s own gendered laws, workplaces, families, and media is itself ethnocentric.

Chapter synthesis

Gender is simultaneously personal and institutional. Bodies matter, but bodies do not arrive with complete social instructions. Families begin the earliest lessons; schools, peers, media, workplaces, law, religion, and markets repeat or revise them. Those lessons shape identity and expression, but also time, money, care, safety, authority, and opportunity. Sociological analysis asks not only how people differ, but how societies assign meaning to difference—and how families and institutions can prepare every child to participate with competence, dignity, and purpose.

Study aid

Key terms

  • Chromosome: A structure containing DNA.
  • Karyotype: An organized image of chromosomes.
  • X-bearing sperm: A sperm cell carrying an X chromosome.
  • Y-bearing sperm: A sperm cell carrying a Y chromosome.
  • Sex characteristics: Chromosomal, hormonal, gonadal, anatomical, and physical traits associated with sex development.
  • Differences of sex development: Variations in chromosomes, genes, hormones, or anatomy that do not follow typical pathways.
  • Gender identity: A person’s internal understanding of gender.
  • Gender expression: The social communication of gender.
  • Gender role: A social expectation attached to a gender category.
  • Gender ideology: Beliefs that explain or justify gender arrangements.
  • Patriarchy: A social system in which men as a group disproportionately hold authority and resources.
  • Shettles method: An unsupported timing theory for attempting to select a baby’s sex.
  • Sex ratio at birth: The number of male births relative to female births in a population.
  • Gender-biased sex selection: Practices intended to affect a child’s sex because one sex is socially preferred.
  • Son preference: A social system that gives greater value, resources, or status to sons.
  • Trisomy: Three copies of a particular chromosome.
  • Monosomy: One copy of a chromosome instead of the usual pair.
  • Assent: A child’s affirmative agreement to care or research when the child cannot yet provide full legal consent.
  • Medical paternalism: Professionals overriding or limiting patient choice because they believe they know what is best.
  • Bodily autonomy: A person’s right to participate in decisions about their own body.
  • Gender socialization: Learning a society’s gender meanings, expectations, and practices.
  • Hidden curriculum: Unwritten lessons communicated through routines, rewards, discipline, and representation.
  • Doing gender: Producing and being held accountable to gender in everyday interaction.
  • Intersectionality: Analysis of how systems such as gender, race, and class combine.
  • Second shift: Unpaid household and care labor performed after paid work.
  • Mental load: The work of anticipating, remembering, and coordinating household needs.
  • Matrilineal: Tracing descent or inheritance through the maternal line.
  • Matrifocal: Family organization in which mothers or women are central to everyday kinship life.
  • Separate spheres: An ideology assigning men to public life and women to domestic life.

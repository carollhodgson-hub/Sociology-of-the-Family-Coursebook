Section overview

Families do not simply begin; they reorganize

A life-course perspective studies timing, transitions, historical context, and linked lives: what happens to one family member changes the opportunities, demands, and meanings experienced by others.

Explain separation and divorce as legal, economic, emotional, parental, and community processes.

Evaluate how gender, class, race, health, work history, policy, and geography shape later life.

Connection to the book’s theme: preparing people for adult life includes preparing them for change—relationship transitions, aging, care, grief, and renegotiated roles. Guidance is not control; it is the practice of teaching coping, communication, flexibility, and help-seeking before a crisis arrives.
8.1 Separation and divorce

One legal event can contain many social transitions

Separation begins when partners stop organizing daily life as a couple; divorce legally ends a marriage. Neither is a single emotional moment. People may experience overlapping emotional, legal, economic, coparental, community, and psychic divorces. The sequence and timing differ, and not every family passes through every stage.

Macro forces
Family law, employment, housing, childcare, healthcare, gender inequality, cultural acceptance, and safety resources shape what choices are possible.
Micro processes
Communication, conflict, betrayal, changing expectations, stress, health, intimacy, and individual meaning shape lived experience.
Children’s adjustment
Risk is patterned, not predetermined. Persistent conflict, instability, economic loss, unsafe behavior, and disrupted parenting matter; warm relationships and predictable routines can protect.
Safety exception
Cooperative coparenting is not appropriate when contact enables coercive control or danger. Safety planning and court orders require qualified local guidance.

No-fault divorce: changing the exit rules

Fault divorce requires proof of legally recognized marital wrongdoing. No-fault divorce allows dissolution based on breakdown, incompatibility, or irreconcilable differences without proving blame. Unilateral divorce means one spouse can obtain a divorce without the other spouse’s consent. The terms overlap, but they are not identical. All states now recognize a no-fault route; some also retain fault grounds, while “pure” no-fault systems do not. The number labeled pure no-fault can differ across legal summaries because statutes, waiting periods, and definitions change.

Reform expanded individual exit options and reduced the need to stage or prove private misconduct in court. This was especially consequential when economic dependence, stigma, or violence made mutual consent difficult. At the same time, a formally equal right to leave does not ensure equal post-divorce resources: earnings, caregiving history, legal representation, housing, child support, property rules, and safety still shape outcomes.

8.2 Remarriage

Repartnering joins histories—not blank slates

Remarriage may bring affection, companionship, financial cooperation, and renewed commitment. It can also connect existing children, former partners, grandparents, debts, property, grief, parenting routines, and legal responsibilities. Sociologists therefore study a remarriage chain: relationships from earlier unions remain socially relevant even when a new union begins.

Boundary ambiguity
Who is “inside” the family, who decides, and who owes what to whom may be unclear.
Incomplete institutionalization
Stepfamily roles have fewer shared rules and rituals than first-marriage roles, so members must negotiate more.
Economic complexity
Child support, assets, debts, inheritance, benefits, and obligations across households may compete.
Loyalty binds
A child may fear that liking a stepparent betrays a parent; adults can make room for multiple attachments.

8.3 Stepfamilies

Build relationships before demanding family feelings

A stepfamily forms when at least one adult has a child from a previous relationship. Members may live together full-time, part-time, or not at all. A household chart therefore cannot capture the whole binuclear or multihousehold family system.

Types of stepfamilies

Stepfamilies vary according to which adults bring children, where children live, whether the couple has a child together, and whether previous partners remain part of the parenting network. The categories can overlap.

Simple stepfamily

Only one adult brings a child or children from a previous relationship. The other adult becomes a stepparent.

Complex or blended stepfamily

Both adults bring children from earlier relationships, creating stepsibling and multiple-household ties.

“Brady Bunch” stepfamily

An informal name for a complex family in which both partners bring children. Real families rarely blend as quickly as television suggests.

Stepfamily with a mutual child

The couple has a child together, creating half-sibling ties and a member biologically connected to both sides.

Resident stepfamily

A child lives primarily or substantially with a parent and stepparent.

Nonresident stepfamily

A child lives mainly elsewhere but remains part of the family through parenting time, communication, support, and ritual.

8.4 Aging and later-life families

Aging is biological, social, unequal, and meaningful

Gerontology is the study of aging and later life. Chronological age matters, but health, disability, work, wealth, gender, race and ethnicity, immigration, neighborhood, family structure, and public policy shape how aging is experienced. Labels such as young-old (often 65–74), old-old (75–84), and oldest-old (85+) are descriptive conventions—not diagnoses.

Life-course theory: middle and later adulthood are connected

Life-course theory does not divide everyone into rigid age boxes. It asks how historical time and place, timing, linked lives, human agency, and cumulative advantage or disadvantage shape pathways. A layoff at 52, caregiving at 61, retirement at 67, widowhood at 76, or disability at 84 has different meanings because each transition occurs within a distinct biography and network.

Theories are lenses, not competing personality labels

Activity theory

Well-being is supported when people replace lost roles with meaningful participation, relationships, and purpose. Activity must be accessible and chosen—not compulsory busyness.

Disengagement theory

Society and older adults gradually withdraw from one another, allowing role transfer. The theory is historically important but criticized for treating exclusion as natural and overlooking involuntary isolation.

Continuity theory

People adapt by preserving familiar identities, values, abilities, relationships, and strategies. Continuity can be internal—sense of self—or external—places, routines, and networks.

Social exchange theory

Power and dependence shift with resources such as income, housing, health, knowledge, transportation, care, affection, and time. Exchanges can be delayed and symbolic, not merely equal trades.

Life-course theory

Later life reflects accumulated opportunities and constraints, cohort history, transition timing, agency, and the linked decisions of family members.

Conflict theory

Class, racism, sexism, ageism, work histories, and policy distribute pensions, healthcare, safe housing, time, and longevity unequally.

Activity theory: meaningful participation and health

Activity theory argues that satisfaction in later life is supported when people sustain or replace valued roles, relationships, movement, and purpose. Retirement may remove daily structure; widowhood may reduce companionship; inaccessible transportation may close off community participation. New roles can restore connection. Research often finds associations between social participation, physical activity, cognitive stimulation, and health, but the relationship is bidirectional: declining health can reduce activity, and reduced opportunity can contribute to further isolation or deconditioning.

Disengagement theory: withdrawal and role transfer

Cumming and Henry proposed that aging involves a mutual, gradual withdrawal: older adults reduce participation while institutions transfer authority and work roles to younger people. In the theory, retirement can provide an orderly way for new workers to enter and organizations to renew leadership. Critics argue that the model confuses chosen change with exclusion, treats one Western pattern as universal, and can justify age discrimination. Many older adults seek continued work or new forms of contribution.

Social exchange theory: relationships contain resources and power

Exchange theory studies how people give and receive valued resources—money, time, care, housing, transportation, knowledge, affection, status, and decision-making support. Reciprocity does not require an immediate equal trade. A grandparent may provide years of childcare and later receive transportation or care. Problems arise when one person’s dependence is used to control choices, or when an institution recognizes financial resources but ignores unpaid care and knowledge.

Continuity theory: adapt the method, preserve the self

Continuity theory proposes that adults cope with change by maintaining patterns that support a coherent identity. Internal continuity includes values, temperament, memories, skills, and preferred coping strategies. External continuity includes familiar places, activities, relationships, possessions, and routines. Continuity is not refusing change: a runner may become a walker, a gardener may use raised beds, or a musician may join an accessible community ensemble.

Conflict theory: later life reflects accumulated inequality

Conflict theory asks who controls valued resources and whose needs are treated as private family problems. Later-life inequality builds through unequal wages, occupational hazards, unpaid caregiving, housing discrimination, racialized wealth gaps, disability barriers, and uneven access to pensions and healthcare. Women and marginalized groups may enter later life with fewer assets after a lifetime of lower pay or interrupted employment. Policies governing Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, housing, paid leave, transportation, and long-term care redistribute both security and risk.

Cumulative inequality
Small disadvantages in schooling, wages, health exposure, and savings can compound across decades.
Gendered care labor
Unpaid care sustains families and society but may reduce earnings, retirement contributions, and time for health.
Political power
Budgets and eligibility rules decide whether care is public infrastructure, employer responsibility, or unpaid family work.
Intersectionality
Age interacts with class, race, gender, disability, citizenship, sexuality, and geography rather than operating alone.

Ageism: when assumptions become barriers

Ageism includes stereotypes (beliefs), prejudice (feelings or evaluations), and discrimination (actions, rules, or practices) based on age. It can target people at any age, although this chapter focuses on older adults. Ageism may be hostile—“older people are a burden”—or seemingly caring, such as automatically speaking for an older adult or taking over a task without asking.

1. StereotypeA belief such as “people over 70 cannot learn new technology.”
2. PrejudiceA judgment or feeling, such as impatience or distrust, based on that belief.
3. Interpersonal discriminationAn action, such as excluding a qualified person from training because of age.
4. Institutional ageismRules, designs, or routines that repeatedly disadvantage an age group.
An attitude is not identical to an act of discrimination—but it is not harmless. A person can notice an automatic age-based thought and choose not to act on it. Yet unexamined attitudes can influence tone, whom we consult, which risks we notice, and which policies feel “normal.” Reflection creates a pause in which evidence, consent, and the individual person can replace the stereotype.

8.5 Grandparenting

Grandparents occupy styles and roles that can change

Classic typologies describe recurring patterns of interaction, not fixed kinds of people. Culture, distance, health, work, family conflict, technology, parental boundaries, and children’s ages can shift a grandparent’s role. A grandparent may be playful during a visit, preserve family history, provide daily care, and later need care.

Formal

Warm and interested while respecting parents’ authority and established boundaries.

Fun-seeker

Emphasizes play, leisure, outings, and an informal relationship.

Reservoir of family wisdom

Transmits stories, ancestry, culture, values, skills, or spiritual traditions.

Distant figure

Has limited contact because of geography, conflict, health, boundaries, or preference.

Surrogate or custodial parent

Assumes substantial or primary parenting responsibility; may need legal, financial, school, and health supports.

Companionate or buddy

Builds an affectionate, supportive relationship through conversation and shared interests.

Grandparents or other relatives raising children may need kinship-care navigation, respite, income support, legal authority, school access, healthcare coordination, and trauma-informed services. 8.6 Caregiving, loss, and family adaptation

Care is love, labor, skill, time, and policy

Family caregivers may provide transportation, meals, bathing, medication organization, advocacy, financial management, supervision, emotional reassurance, and coordination with professionals. Care can be meaningful and exhausting at the same time. The sandwich generation supports younger and older relatives simultaneously, often while employed.

Routine care
Expected, recurring help with everyday needs.
Backup care
Substitute or emergency support when the primary plan fails.
Circumscribed care
Responsibility for a particular task such as bills, meals, or transportation.
Sporadic care
Irregular help when needs arise.

Death, dying, and bereavement

Families may face anticipatory grief before a death, bereavement afterward, and practical work involving care decisions, rituals, finances, and reorganized roles. Hospice focuses on comfort and quality of life near the end of life; palliative care can address symptoms and quality of life alongside treatment at many stages of serious illness. Grief does not follow one universal set of stages. Cultural practices, relationship histories, circumstances of death, and social support shape mourning.

Ask a better sociological question: instead of “Why can’t this caregiver cope?” ask “What combination of tasks, relationships, work rules, healthcare access, money, respite, and public policy is producing the strain—and what resources could change it?”

Key concepts

Language for studying family change

  • Life-course perspective: analysis of timing, transitions, historical context, agency, and linked lives.
  • Linked lives: principle that individual life pathways are interdependent.
  • Separation: transition in which partners stop organizing life as a couple, whether or not a legal divorce follows.
  • Divorce: legal dissolution of marriage.
  • Crude divorce rate: divorces per 1,000 people in the total population during a period.
  • Refined divorce rate: divorces relative to the married population at risk during a period.
  • Coparenting: ways adults coordinate responsibility for a child across or within households.
  • Remarriage chain: continuing network of obligations and relationships connecting current and prior unions.
  • Boundary ambiguity: uncertainty about family membership, authority, or obligations.
  • Stepfamily: family in which at least one adult has a child from a previous relationship.
  • Simple stepfamily: stepfamily in which only one partner brings a child or children from an earlier relationship.
  • Complex or blended stepfamily: stepfamily in which both partners bring children from earlier relationships.
  • Mutual child: child born to or adopted by the current couple who also has half-sibling ties to children from earlier relationships.
  • Binuclear family: post-separation family system organized across two parental households.
  • Loyalty bind: felt pressure that closeness to one family member betrays another.
  • Gerontology: interdisciplinary study of aging and later life.
  • Cumulative advantage or disadvantage: process through which resources and risks build across time, producing unequal later-life outcomes.
  • Transition timing: when a life event occurs relative to age, other roles, historical conditions, and socially expected schedules.
  • Ageism: stereotyping, prejudice, or discrimination based on age.
  • Internalized ageism: applying age stereotypes learned from society to oneself and one’s own aging.
  • Institutional ageism: policies, designs, or routine practices that systematically disadvantage people because of age.
  • Elderspeak: patronizing or childlike speech directed toward an older adult based on assumptions about competence.
  • Activity theory: view emphasizing meaningful engagement in later-life satisfaction.
  • Disengagement theory: theory proposing mutual withdrawal between older people and society.
  • Continuity theory: view that adaptation often preserves identities and established patterns.
  • Internal continuity: preservation of identity, values, preferences, and coping strategies.
  • External continuity: preservation or adaptation of environments, activities, routines, and relationships.
  • Exchange theory: analysis of resources, reciprocity, dependence, and power.
  • Grandparenting style: recurring pattern of interaction with grandchildren; not a fixed diagnosis.
  • Custodial grandparent: grandparent with substantial or primary responsibility for raising a grandchild.
  • Sandwich generation: adults supporting younger and older relatives at the same time.
  • Caregiver strain: physical, emotional, social, or financial pressure associated with care demands.
  • Respite: temporary relief for a caregiver.
  • Anticipatory grief: grief occurring before an expected loss.
  • Bereavement: condition and adjustment period following a death.
